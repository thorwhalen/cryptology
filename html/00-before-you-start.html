Before you start

How this book works, and how to use it

This book teaches you how codebreakers think: how to look at a page of scrambled letters, work out what kind of cipher made it, and then break it, with nothing more than a pencil, some squared paper and your own head.

Who it is for

It is written for curious 11 to 13 year olds, the kind who enjoy math competitions and puzzles, and for anyone older who wants the same ideas without the fuss. You do not need to know any cryptography. You also do not need any math beyond arithmetic: every grown-up idea (clock arithmetic, the sigma sign, a little probability) is introduced when it is needed, in plain words first, then in the real notation, then with a worked example.

How the book is built

There are nine guides. Each one teaches one method of attack, and each one stands on its own, so you can open the book at any guide. They all have the same shape:

  1. The big idea, in one paragraph and a picture.
  2. Which ciphers it breaks, and which it does not.
  3. Why it works, with a small experiment you could do by hand.
  4. The grown-up version: the notation and words that real cryptanalysts use.
  5. A worked example, start to finish, with every number shown.
  6. When to try it, and when not to.
  7. Traps and tips.
  8. By hand or by computer: the jobs the method involves, roughly how long each takes by hand, and the tool that does it for you.
  9. Try it yourself: a few exercises, with the answers at the end of the guide.
  10. Words to know, and Going further.

All the words from every guide are collected again, A to Z, at the back of the book.

Brain, pencil or computer?

This book teaches the methods the classical way, but it does not ask you to do everything by hand forever. Do each mechanical job by hand once, so you know what a machine does when it does it for you; after that, let the machine do it, and spend your time on the thinking. The next chapter, Brain, pencil or computer?, explains why, and what that choice costs.

Which order to read them in

Read Brain, pencil or computer? first: it is short. Then start with Guide 1, Reading the clues. It shows how to tell, before trying a single key, which family a cipher belongs to, and the other guides build on that habit. After that, follow what interests you:

If you want to break Read
Caesar, affine and substitution ciphers Guides 2 and 3, then 6
Vigenère and other ciphers with several alphabets Guides 4 and 5, then 6
Ciphers that move letters instead of changing them Guides 7, then 6
Big ciphers by computer, and ciphers that work on pairs of letters Guides 8 and 9

The app that goes with it

The book has a companion website, the cryptology app, at apps.thorwhalen.com/cryptology. It has tools for measuring and cracking a ciphertext, exercises with fresh texts every time, a family tree of ciphers, and a glossary. A word about competitions

Codebreaking competitions, such as the UK’s National Cipher Challenge, have rules about tools. In a live round of the National Cipher Challenge, online cipher and code-breaking tools and AI are against the rules, and the work must be the team’s own. This book, its exercises and its app are for learning and for practice. When you enter a competition, read its rules and follow them.

For the adult helping

The best way to use this book with a young reader is to let them do the work. Read a guide’s big idea together, then hand over the pencil for the worked example and the exercises. Ask questions rather than giving answers: “What did you count?” and “What would English look like here?” are more useful than any hint. Breaks help: forty minutes of real concentration is a lot.
